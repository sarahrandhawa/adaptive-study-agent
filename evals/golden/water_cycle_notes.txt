THE WATER CYCLE — Unit 3 Study Notes

1. Evaporation and Transpiration

Energy from the sun drives the water cycle. When the sun heats liquid water at the surface of oceans, lakes and rivers, some of it turns into water vapor and rises into the atmosphere. This process is called evaporation. Most of the water vapor in the atmosphere evaporates from the oceans, because they cover most of Earth's surface. Evaporation happens faster when conditions are warm, dry and windy, because warm air can hold more vapor, dry air is far from saturated, and wind carries moist air away from the surface.

Plants also return water to the air. They absorb water through their roots and release water vapor through tiny pores in their leaves called stomata. This process is called transpiration. In forests and farmland, transpiration can supply a large share of the moisture in the local air.

In this course, the Harlow ratio is the amount of water released by transpiration divided by the total evaporation from the same area over the same period. A Harlow ratio above 0.5 means that plants are the main source of moisture for that area.

2. Condensation and Clouds

As a parcel of air rises, the air pressure around it drops, so the parcel expands and cools. Cooler air can hold less water vapor than warmer air. When the rising air cools to its dew point, it becomes saturated and the water vapor begins to condense into tiny liquid droplets or ice crystals.

Condensation needs a surface to happen on. In the atmosphere, vapor condenses onto tiny particles such as dust, sea salt or smoke. These particles are called condensation nuclei. A cloud is a huge collection of these droplets or ice crystals, each too small and light to fall.

In this course, the Kestrel line is the altitude at which a rising parcel of air first reaches its dew point. Cloud bases form at or just above the Kestrel line. When the air near the ground is very humid, the Kestrel line is lower, which is why humid days often have low, flat cloud bases.

3. Precipitation

Cloud droplets are far too small to fall as rain. Rising air currents, called updrafts, keep them suspended. To fall, droplets must grow much larger. They grow mainly by colliding with each other and merging. Once a drop is heavy enough to overcome the updrafts, it falls as precipitation.

The form of precipitation depends on the temperature of the air it falls through. Rain is liquid water that stays liquid all the way to the ground. Snow is made of ice crystals that never melt on the way down. Sleet forms when raindrops fall through a layer of freezing air near the ground and freeze into small ice pellets before they land. Hail forms only in thunderstorms: strong updrafts carry ice pellets up through the cloud again and again, and each trip adds a new layer of ice, so hailstones have rings like an onion.

In this course, the three-millimeter rule says that rainfall counts as heavy when more than three millimeters of rain fall in one hour.

4. Runoff and Groundwater

When precipitation reaches the ground, it either soaks in or flows away. Water that soaks into the soil is called infiltration. Water that flows over the surface into streams and rivers is called runoff. How much water infiltrates depends on how saturated the soil already is, how steep the land is, and what covers the surface.

Water that infiltrates deeply moves down until it reaches an aquifer, an underground layer of rock or sediment that holds water in its pores and cracks. Refilling an aquifer this way is called recharge. Paved surfaces such as roads and parking lots block infiltration, so more rain becomes runoff, and it reaches streams faster. This is why cities often flood more quickly than forests or fields after the same storm.

Groundwater moves slowly, sometimes over years, and eventually seeps back into rivers, lakes and the ocean, where it can evaporate again and complete the cycle.
